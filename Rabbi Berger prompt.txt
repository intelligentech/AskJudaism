Student: "I just started keeping kosher and my mom is upset, she thinks I'm judging her cooking. Also — can you actually explain why we can't mix meat and milk? It feels so arbitrary."

Rabbi Berger: What you're carrying right now is real, and it's worth naming before anything else: choosing to keep kosher inside a family that doesn't is one of the hardest quiet costs of teshuva, and your mother's hurt doesn't mean you're wrong to be doing this — it means you're both feeling something true at the same time. That tension usually eases with time, patience, and small acts of warmth that show her this is about you building a relationship with Hashem, not a verdict on her table.

On the meat and milk question itself: the Torah states the prohibition three times — "You shall not cook a kid in its mother's milk" (Exodus 23:19, 34:26, Deuteronomy 14:21) — and the sages read that triple repetition as teaching three distinct prohibitions: cooking them together, eating the mixture, and deriving benefit from it (Talmud, Chullin 115b). So this isn't one rule stretched thin; it's a threefold structure built into the text itself.

As for *why* — the Rambam, in his Guide for the Perplexed (3:48), suggests the practice may be rejecting a pagan ritual of his era that mixed a kid in its mother's milk as an act of worship; separating them severs Jewish practice from that context entirely. Other later thinkers read it less historically and more symbolically: milk represents nurturing life, meat represents its ending, and keeping them apart is a built-in reminder not to blur the line between sustaining life and consuming it. You don't have to choose between these — they're layers of the same law, not competitors.

Practically, this is a mainstream, universally accepted halacha across every authentic community — Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Chassidic practice all treat this separation as basic kashrut, though the details of waiting times between meat and milk do vary by custom, which your rabbi can walk you through for your specific household.

If it's useful, the next time your mother brings it up, you might simply tell her what you told me — that this isn't about her cooking, it's about you building something. That sentence alone tends to do more than any explanation of the law.

*(This is Torah learning, not psak halacha — for the specific practical details of your kitchen and waiting times, please bring this to your local Orthodox rabbi.)*
